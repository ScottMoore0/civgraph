Words in parliament

How often a word or phrase was used in the Dáil, the Seanad and the Northern Ireland Assembly, and by Irish and Northern Ireland members at Westminster, from 1803 to 2026: year by year, by chamber, by party and by speaker. Counts come from the official reports of about 855 million spoken words.

    Who said it most

    The thirty speakers who used the term most, across all the chambers, for those with at least 5,000 words on record. Rates are uses per 10,000 of the speaker's own words in that chamber; the rate column ranks only speakers with 20,000 words or more.

    Which parties said it

    For one term and chamber over the years shown: each party's share of the uses against its members' share of all the words spoken. A ratio above 1 means the party's members used the term more than their share of the debate would predict.

    How this is counted

    • Words. Each speech is split into lower-case words of letters and digits, keeping an apostrophe inside a word (don't, O'Brien). Accents are kept and nothing is stemmed, so house and houses are different terms. Two-word phrases are counted within a paragraph.
    • Rates. A rate is uses per million words spoken in the same chamber, year, party and language. When lines combine chambers or parties, the uses and the words are each added up first, so the rate is never an average of rates. Smoothing does the same over a window of years.
    • Stop words and cut-offs. Common words such as the and of (187 English and 81 Irish stop words) are not searchable on their own, nor are phrases made only of them. A single word is kept if it was used at least 10 times in that language; a two-word phrase if it was used at least 100 times. Speaker lists count speakers with at least 5,000 words.
    • Irish. The language is decided paragraph by paragraph and is approximate; Irish counts are kept apart from English.
    • Who is included at Westminster. Commons speeches count only when the speaker held an Irish or Northern Ireland seat in Civgraph's election record on that date; the Lords set is 36 peers linked to Northern Ireland, from 1999. From 1803 to 1918 a printed speaker label (such as "MR. PARNELL") is counted only when exactly one Irish member sitting that day fits it and no member for a British seat does, so Irish members' words before 1919 are a lower bound; 167 volumes are also missing from the Hansard archive, which thins the 1890s.
    • Reading the chart. A gap means no sittings, or no members in scope, that year. A dotted stretch means fewer than 100,000 words were spoken that year in the chambers shown, so one speech can swing the rate. Older reports were scanned, so expect some misread words before the 1990s.
    • Parties are as each source records them at the date of the speech, grouped under Civgraph's party records where one exists (so DUP and Democratic Unionist Party are one line).
